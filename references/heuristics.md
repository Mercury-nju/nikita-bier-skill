# Product Heuristics

Use these heuristics in order. The point is not to "sound like Nikita." The point is to reason the way his public work repeatedly suggests.

## Quick Scoring

Before going deep, score the idea from `0-2` on each axis:

- `Demand`: is there a strong human motive?
- `Orchestration`: can the first payoff be created reliably?
- `Distribution`: is there a native pull for another person?
- `Density`: is there a tight initial graph?
- `Shareability`: is there something worth forwarding or screenshotting?
- `Durability`: is there a reason to come back after novelty?

Fast interpretation:

- `0-4`: mostly dead
- `5-7`: interesting but structurally weak
- `8-10`: worth testing
- `11-12`: unusually strong

Then explain which axis is the bottleneck. Do not just report the score.

## 1. Name The Primitive Motivation

Consumer products usually win because they attach to a deep recurring motive, not because they are functionally complete.

Look first for:

- status
- belonging
- validation
- curiosity
- gossip
- romance
- money
- escape

Questions to ask:

- What emotion gets someone to open this immediately?
- What social outcome does the user want from this?
- What would make this feel urgent instead of merely useful?

## 2. Test Whether The Value State Is Orchestratable

Before asking how it spreads, check whether the product can produce value on demand.

Questions:

- What exact conditions are required for the first "aha"?
- How many other people have to cooperate?
- Can a normal user create those conditions repeatedly?

Red flag:

- the product only works in a perfect social scenario that the team cannot reliably trigger

## 3. Judge the Product by Its Distribution Primitive

Do not ask only "Is this good?" Ask "How does this spread?"

Look for:

- direct invites
- asymmetric information
- reputation loops
- school, campus, or group density
- reasons to screenshot, quote, or forward
- repeated social check-ins

Red flag:

- the product is useful but there is no native reason to pull another person in

## 4. Compress Time-to-Value

One of the strongest public themes in Nikita Bier material is that value has to land almost immediately.

Questions:

- Can the user understand the payoff in seconds?
- Are we asking for setup before dopamine?
- Can we invert the sequence and show reward first?

Good pattern:

```text
show payoff
then request action
then deepen the loop
```

Bad pattern:

```text
permissions
profile setup
tutorial
empty product
```

## 5. Prefer Dense Networks Over Broad Abstraction

Many consumer products die because they launch too horizontally.

Look for a tightly connected graph:

- a school
- a friend group
- a city scene
- a niche fandom
- a work cohort with shared context

Questions:

- Where is the smallest network where this feels socially mandatory?
- What group would feel left out if they were not on it?

## 6. Reproducible Testing Beats Vision Theater

When choosing what to improve, privilege testing throughput.

Ask:

- can we run more shots this week?
- are we learning from a repeated loop or a one-off anecdote?
- are we polishing narratives instead of increasing experiments?

Default move:

- shorten cycle time before expanding scope

## 7. Latent Demand Beats Survey Demand

Public interviews repeatedly point to "people already doing this inefficiently" as a strong signal.

Look for:

- ugly workarounds
- ritualized screenshots
- private group chats doing a job badly
- spreadsheets or Notes apps used as social infrastructure

If users are already hacking together a behavior, the demand may be real even if they never asked for the product in words.

## 8. Optimize for Conversation, Not Just Retention

Some products survive because they create reasons to talk, not just reasons to click.

Ask:

- Does this create a social situation?
- Does it produce a story users want to tell other people?
- Is the output visible, discussable, or scoreable?

## 9. Avoid Fragmentation Before Density

Early communities usually need concentration more than customization.

Ask:

- are we splitting identity, feed, or namespace too early?
- does each extra layer reduce content density?
- are we increasing cognitive load before social reward?

Default move:

- centralize attention before adding subworlds

## 10. Distinguish Viral From Durable

A recurring public stance is that viral is learnable, durable is much harder.

So split the judgment:

- Viral potential: can this spike?
- Durability potential: what keeps this meaningful after novelty fades?

Use both. Do not confuse short-term spread with lasting product strength.

## 11. Product Strategy Is Often Emotion Strategy

For consumer and social products, the real lever is often not more features. It is cleaner emotional design.

Useful rewrites:

- "This needs more features" -> "This needs a stronger social emotion"
- "This is useful" -> "This lacks urgency"
- "People like it" -> "They still do not need to tell anyone"

## 12. When Advising, Be Blunt but Concrete

The distilled voice should cut quickly to the real issue:

```text
This is a tool, not a social situation.
The onboarding is eating the dopamine.
The product has utility but no invitation vector.
The first meaningful moment is too hard to orchestrate.
This might retain, but it won't spread.
```

Then immediately explain the mechanism and the fix.
